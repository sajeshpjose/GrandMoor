How to Improve Order Accuracy in Your Warehouse

By Grandmoor Inc.

A customer may forgive a delayed shipment once. Sending the wrong item, quantity, color, or configuration is harder to recover from. It creates return costs, customer-service work, inventory discrepancies, and lost confidence in your operation. Knowing how to improve order accuracy starts with treating every warehouse handoff as part of the customer experience, not as a separate back-room task.

For importers, e-commerce brands, wholesalers, and distributors, accuracy is rarely solved by asking pickers to work harder. Most errors come from unclear processes, poor inventory discipline, confusing locations, or missing verification points. The right approach is practical: identify where errors begin, build controls into the workflow, and measure performance closely enough to correct problems before they become recurring costs.

Start by Finding Where Errors Enter the Process

An incorrect order is often the final symptom of an earlier problem. A SKU may have been received under the wrong item number, put away in the wrong slot, or recorded with the wrong available quantity. By the time a picker reaches the shelf, the system may already be directing them toward a mistake.

Track exceptions by process stage: receiving, putaway, replenishment, picking, packing, shipping, and returns. Do not rely only on a total order accuracy percentage. A 98% rate may sound acceptable until a business shipping 10,000 orders per month realizes it still means 200 incorrect shipments.

Review each exception for its root cause. Was the order picked correctly but packed with the wrong item? Did the warehouse system show stock that was not physically available? Did two nearly identical products share a location? The answer determines the fix. More final inspection will not solve a receiving error, and new scanning equipment will not help if product master data is inconsistent.

Improve Receiving Before Inventory Reaches the Shelf

Order accuracy begins when freight arrives. Receiving teams need a defined process for matching incoming product against purchase orders, bills of lading, packing lists, and expected SKU details. Count units, inspect labels, record shortages or damage, and separate questionable inventory before it is made available for fulfillment.

This matters especially for containerized imports and mixed-SKU shipments. A rushed unload can create problems that stay hidden until customer orders are picked days or weeks later. During container destuffing, count and identify goods as they are unloaded, not after pallets have been mixed into the warehouse flow.

Use a receiving status that prevents inventory from being allocated until it has been checked and entered correctly. For some products, a full count is appropriate. For sealed cases from a reliable supplier, a documented case-count process may be sufficient. The right level of verification depends on product value, supplier consistency, packaging quality, and the cost of an error.

Build Inventory Control Into Daily Warehouse Work

Accurate orders require accurate available inventory. That means the physical count, warehouse management system, and sales channels must agree on what is on hand, where it is stored, and whether it can be allocated.

Cycle counting is more effective than waiting for an annual physical inventory. Count high-volume, high-value, and error-prone SKUs more often. If a fast-moving item is counted weekly, an inventory issue can be corrected before it causes a string of backorders or substitutions. Slow-moving, stable products may require less frequent review.

Location discipline is just as important. Every pickable SKU should have an assigned location that is clearly labeled and reflected in the system. Avoid placing similar items, colors, sizes, or revisions beside one another when possible. If products must be adjacent, use clear visual differentiation and separate bin labels. A picker working at speed should not need to guess which version is correct.

Inventory changes also need authorization and a record. Adjustments for damage, shortages, samples, internal use, or returns should be documented with a reason code. Unexplained adjustments hide process failures and make it difficult to know whether inventory data can be trusted.

Make the Pick Path Easy to Follow

A well-designed pick process reduces the number of decisions a warehouse associate has to make. The order should clearly show the SKU, description, quantity, location, and any special handling requirement. If your operation uses barcode scanning, require a scan confirmation at the location and product level rather than allowing a picker to confirm from memory.

For businesses without a warehouse management system, disciplined paper or digital pick tickets can still work at lower volume. The key is consistency. Each order needs a traceable path from release through shipment, with clear confirmation that the right product and quantity were selected.

Batch picking can improve productivity, but it introduces risks when many orders are handled at once. Use separate totes, carts, or compartments for each order and label them before picking begins. Zone picking can also be effective in larger facilities, provided handoffs between zones are scanned or otherwise verified.

The best method depends on order volume and product profile. Single-order picking may be slower but easier to control for high-value, complex orders. Batch or wave picking may make more sense for a large number of small, similar e-commerce orders. The process should match the operation, not force every client into the same warehouse model.

Add Verification at Packing, Not Just at Shipping

Packing is the last practical opportunity to catch most fulfillment errors. The pack station should confirm the order contents before labels are applied and cartons are closed. Ideally, the person packing verifies the picker’s work rather than simply repeating the same assumption.

A reliable packing check includes confirming SKU, quantity, order number, and any required inserts, bundles, lot codes, or documentation. For parcel shipments, a scale can provide another useful control. If the expected order weight differs materially from the packed carton weight, the shipment should be reviewed before it leaves the building.

Do not overbuild the process for every order. A second-person check on every low-value, single-SKU shipment may add labor without a meaningful benefit. Reserve additional verification for high-value orders, multi-line orders, regulated products, custom kits, and customers with strict routing or compliance requirements.

Standardize Product Data and Customer Instructions

Warehouse execution is only as accurate as the information provided. Product records should use one clear SKU per sellable item, with consistent descriptions, units of measure, dimensions, and barcodes. If a case, inner pack, and individual unit can all be sold, define each level clearly so receiving and fulfillment teams do not make assumptions.

Customer instructions should be documented in the same way. This includes order cutoffs, carrier preferences, carton rules, labeling requirements, inserts, kitting steps, lot rotation, and exceptions. Instructions stored in scattered emails or held by one employee create avoidable risk when volume increases or staff changes.

For outsourced fulfillment, defined service-level agreements are particularly useful. They establish what accuracy means, what information the client must provide, how exceptions are handled, and when orders must be released to meet dispatch commitments. Clear expectations protect both the client and the warehouse team.

Train for Exceptions, Then Measure the Results

Most warehouse teams understand normal orders. Errors often happen when something is unusual: a short pick, a damaged unit, a missing barcode, a customer-requested substitution, or a product that does not match its system description. Train staff on what to do when the process does not match reality. The correct response should be to stop, document, and escalate, not improvise.

Measure order accuracy consistently using shipped orders as the denominator. A basic calculation is correct orders shipped divided by total orders shipped. Then review the errors by client, SKU, associate, shift, order type, and process stage. The goal is accountability without blame. If the same issue appears repeatedly, management should correct the workflow, layout, data, or training that allows it to continue.

Transparent reporting also gives clients a realistic view of performance. They should know what is happening with their goods, including exceptions and corrective actions, rather than receiving only a broad promise that orders are being handled.

Choose a Partner That Can Maintain Control as Volume Changes

Growth can strain order accuracy quickly. More SKUs, more sales channels, seasonal spikes, and added customer requirements all create complexity. A logistics partner should be able to add storage and fulfillment capacity without losing the process controls that protect each order.

For businesses moving inventory through the Greater Toronto Area or connected cross-border lanes, that hands-on control can reduce the gap between what the system says and what the customer receives.

Order accuracy improves when the warehouse makes the correct action the easiest action. Start with the next recurring error, trace it back to its source, and make one durable process change. Those small operational corrections are what keep your supply chain moving as order volume grows.
