Inventory Control Methods That Keep Orders Moving

By Grandmoor Inc.

A stockout on a fast-moving item can cost more than a missed sale. It can delay a customer order, create extra work for the fulfillment team, and leave your purchasing team scrambling to recover. The right inventory control methods give you a practical way to prevent those problems while avoiding the expense of holding more stock than your operation needs.

For importers, e-commerce brands, wholesalers, and growing product businesses, inventory control is not simply counting boxes. It is the operating discipline that connects inbound freight, warehouse storage, order fulfillment, purchasing, and customer delivery. When the process is clear, you know what is on hand, what is committed to orders, what is arriving, and where attention is needed.

What Inventory Control Should Accomplish

Inventory management and inventory control are closely related, but they serve different purposes. Inventory management is the broader planning function: forecasting demand, purchasing products, and setting inventory policies. Inventory control is the day-to-day execution that keeps physical stock and inventory records aligned.

A dependable control process should answer a few basic questions without delay. How many units are available to sell? Where are they stored? Which products need replenishment? Which items are aging, damaged, or at risk of becoming obsolete? If your team cannot answer those questions confidently, decisions about purchasing and fulfillment become guesses.

The best method depends on your SKU count, order volume, lead times, product value, storage requirements, and sales patterns. A distributor with stable B2B orders needs a different approach from an e-commerce company managing seasonal spikes and frequent small-parcel shipments. The goal is not to use every available method. It is to use the right combination consistently.

Core Inventory Control Methods for Growing Businesses

FIFO for perishable, dated, and time-sensitive stock

First in, first out, or FIFO, means the oldest received inventory is picked first. It is common for food, cosmetics, health products, chemicals, and any product with a shelf-life concern. It is also useful for general merchandise because it reduces the chance that older stock gets buried behind newer receipts.

FIFO only works when receiving and putaway are disciplined. Products must be labeled correctly, assigned to the right location, and positioned so warehouse staff can access the oldest stock first. A FIFO policy written on paper will not protect inventory if new pallets are repeatedly placed in front of older ones.

FEFO when expiration dates drive the decision

First expired, first out, or FEFO, is more precise than FIFO. Instead of picking the oldest receipt, the warehouse picks the product with the nearest expiration date. This matters when two shipments of the same item arrive at different times but have different remaining shelf life.

FEFO requires accurate lot, date, and location records. For businesses handling regulated, consumable, or sensitive goods, that added control can reduce write-offs and help maintain traceability. It also gives your sales and operations teams a clearer view of inventory that may need to move quickly.

Reorder points for predictable replenishment

A reorder point establishes the inventory level that triggers a new purchase order or production order. It is usually based on expected demand during supplier lead time, plus safety stock for normal variation.

For example, if a business sells 20 units per day and the supplier takes 15 days to deliver, it needs 300 units just to cover demand during lead time. If demand or lead time can vary, the company may add a safety-stock buffer. The point is not to create a perfect forecast. It is to make replenishment decisions early enough to protect customer service.

Reorder points should not remain fixed forever. Review them after major sales changes, supplier delays, promotions, new customer wins, or shifts in delivery frequency. A reorder point that was sensible six months ago can become too low or too costly as the business changes.

Min-max controls for straightforward purchasing

Min-max inventory control sets a minimum quantity and a maximum quantity for each item. When stock falls below the minimum, the business replenishes enough to return the item to its maximum level.

This method is easy to understand and works well for products with relatively steady movement. It can also simplify purchasing for companies that do not need a complex forecasting model across every SKU. The trade-off is that min-max settings can create excess inventory if the maximum is too high or demand drops unexpectedly. It needs regular review, especially for slower-moving goods.

ABC analysis to focus effort where it pays off

ABC analysis classifies inventory by business impact. A-items are typically high-value or high-volume products that deserve close attention. B-items need normal oversight. C-items are lower-impact products that can often be managed with simpler controls.

The classifications do not have to be based only on unit cost. Sales velocity, margin, customer importance, lead time, and stockout risk can all matter. A low-cost replacement part that stops a major customer order may deserve A-item treatment.

ABC analysis helps teams avoid spending equal time on every SKU. It is usually better to count critical products often, set tighter reorder rules for them, and investigate discrepancies quickly. Less critical inventory still needs control, but it should not consume the same operational attention.

Accuracy Starts at the Receiving Dock

Many inventory problems begin before product reaches a storage location. If a container is destuffed, pallets are received, or cartons are checked in without comparing the physical count and condition against the expected shipment, incorrect records enter the system immediately.

A strong receiving process verifies quantities, identifies visible damage, captures product details, and records exceptions before stock becomes available for fulfillment. Goods should then be labeled and assigned to specific warehouse locations. This creates an audit trail from inbound delivery through outbound order dispatch.

For importers, this discipline can also reduce costly dwell time. When inbound freight is processed promptly and discrepancies are visible early, your team can address supplier issues before they become customer-service issues or long-term storage problems.

Cycle Counts Are Better Than Waiting for an Annual Count

A full physical inventory count has value, but relying on one annual count leaves too much time for errors to build up. Cycle counting checks selected inventory on a regular schedule while normal warehouse activity continues.

A practical cycle count program counts A-items more frequently than B- and C-items. It also targets products with recent discrepancies, high order velocity, frequent returns, or multiple storage locations. When a discrepancy is found, the right response is not merely adjusting the number in the system. Find the cause. It may be a receiving error, an unconfirmed transfer, an incorrect unit of measure, damaged inventory, or a picking issue.

That investigation is what turns counting into control. Over time, recurring causes reveal where training, labeling, warehouse layout, or system processes need improvement.

Match Warehouse Layout to Product Movement

Inventory control methods are harder to maintain in a poorly organized warehouse. Fast-moving products should be accessible to pickers. Heavier or bulk inventory may need pallet storage, while smaller e-commerce units may be better suited to shelving or bin locations. Each location should be clearly identified, and every movement should be recorded.

Slotting should change as demand changes. A product that was once a slow mover may need a more accessible position after a successful retail placement or online promotion. Conversely, stock that has not moved in months should not occupy prime picking space simply because it has always been there.

Use Inventory Data to Make Better Operating Decisions

Accurate data should lead to action. Inventory reports are useful when they show available stock, committed stock, inbound quantities, aging inventory, and movement by SKU. Clear reporting helps operations leaders decide whether to replenish, accelerate sales activity, adjust storage space, or investigate a mismatch.

Avoid treating every item the same. A high-demand SKU with a long overseas lead time needs more safety stock than a locally sourced product that can be replenished within days. Seasonal goods may require planned inventory buildup, followed by a disciplined runout strategy. High-value products may justify tighter access controls and more frequent verification even if their sales volume is low.

The best process is one your team can follow under pressure. If a method is too complicated for receiving staff, warehouse teams, and purchasing managers to use consistently, it will eventually fail when volume rises.

When reviewing your inventory process, start with the products and events that create the most disruption: missed picks, stockouts, receiving discrepancies, aged stock, or delayed replenishment. Put clear controls around those points first, then expand from there. That approach keeps your supply chain moving while giving you a more reliable picture of what is happening with your goods.
