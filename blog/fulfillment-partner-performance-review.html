Fulfillment Partner Performance Review That Works

By Grandmoor Inc.

A missed ship cutoff, an unexplained inventory adjustment, or a growing pile of customer service tickets is not just a warehouse issue. It is a signal that your fulfillment partner performance review needs to go beyond a monthly invoice and a general promise that orders are moving. A useful review shows where the operation is holding up, where risk is building, and what both parties will do next.

For importers, e-commerce brands, wholesalers, and manufacturers, outsourced fulfillment should reduce operational burden without reducing control. The right review process gives your team visibility into the handling of your goods, the accuracy of available inventory, and the service your customers receive after an order is released.

What a fulfillment partner performance review should accomplish

The purpose is not to catch a warehouse making mistakes. Every operation faces exceptions: late inbound containers, damaged cartons, carrier delays, incomplete purchase orders, sudden promotional demand, and products that require special handling. The purpose is to distinguish normal exceptions from recurring process failures.

A productive review answers four practical questions. Are orders leaving accurately and on time? Does the inventory record match the physical stock? Are inbound receipts, returns, and exceptions being handled within agreed timeframes? And when something goes wrong, is the cause visible and the corrective action clear?

That last question matters most. A fulfillment provider can report strong high-level numbers while a few unresolved issues create expensive friction for your business. Clear reporting should make exceptions visible rather than burying them in averages.

Start with the service level agreement

Before evaluating performance, confirm what was actually agreed. A service level agreement should define the operating rules that both teams can measure. If terms are vague, a scorecard will quickly become an argument about expectations instead of a discussion about results.

Review the agreed order cutoff time, same-day or next-day dispatch requirements, receiving turnaround, inventory count frequency, return handling, escalation procedures, and reporting cadence. Also confirm exclusions. A warehouse cannot reasonably meet a same-day shipping target if orders arrive after cutoff, product data is incomplete, or inventory has not been received into available stock.

As volumes change, the SLA may need to change too. A process that works for 30 orders a day may not be sufficient during a retail launch or seasonal peak. Flexible capacity is valuable, but it needs matching labor plans, storage space, carrier coordination, and a shared forecast.

Measure the KPIs that affect customers and cash flow

A good scorecard uses a small set of metrics that connect directly to operational outcomes. Tracking everything can hide the few measures that need action. Review trends over time, not only the latest month, and compare results against the agreed target.

Order accuracy and on-time dispatch

Order accuracy measures whether the correct item, quantity, packaging, and documentation were sent. On-time dispatch measures whether the order left the facility within the promised window. Keep these separate. An order can be accurate but late, or timely but wrong.

Ask for the number of orders processed, the number of errors, the type of error, and the financial or customer impact. A 99.5% accuracy rate may be acceptable for one business and damaging for another. If you ship high-value industrial parts or subscription boxes with strict retailer routing requirements, even a small error rate can carry outsized cost.

Inventory accuracy and adjustment discipline

Inventory accuracy is the foundation of fulfillment. If your available inventory is wrong, your sales team may promise product that cannot ship, purchasing may reorder unnecessarily, and orders may fail at the pack station.

Review system-to-physical accuracy, cycle count results, adjustment volume, adjustment reasons, and aging of unresolved discrepancies. The key is not simply whether adjustments occur. It is whether they are documented, approved, and traced to a cause such as receiving variance, damage, mis-slotting, return processing, or data error.

A partner should be able to explain material adjustments in plain language and show what will prevent repetition. “Inventory was corrected” is not sufficient reporting.

Receiving and putaway turnaround

Inbound delays can quietly create stockouts and demurrage exposure. Measure the time from truck arrival or container destuffing to count verification, system receipt, quality hold if required, and available inventory status.

The right target depends on your freight profile. A mixed container with thousands of loose cartons requires a different plan than palletized replenishment from a domestic supplier. What matters is that the provider flags exceptions quickly: overages, shortages, damage, missing labels, or goods that do not match the advance shipping notice.

Returns, claims, and exception resolution

Returns can reveal product issues, carrier damage, or fulfillment errors. They can also become a black hole when no one defines who inspects, disposes, restocks, or reports them. Review the age of open returns, condition codes, restock timing, and the value of inventory sitting in limbo.

Claims and escalations deserve the same attention. Track the number of open issues, average resolution time, root causes, and ownership. A fast initial response is useful, but a closed issue with no preventive action is likely to reappear.

Look behind the numbers

Metrics tell you where to investigate, not always why performance changed. Use the review meeting to discuss operational conditions that influenced the result. Did order volume spike beyond forecast? Did a new SKU create picking confusion? Were carrier pickups missed? Was inbound freight delivered without an appointment or proper paperwork?

This is where a hands-on provider adds value. Your warehouse contact should understand your account well enough to explain the operational story, not simply forward a dashboard. Direct accountability is especially valuable when your business handles changing product mixes, retailer compliance rules, or cross-border freight moving through the Greater Toronto Area.

Ask for examples of exceptions and how they were managed. A provider that can identify a pattern early, propose a practical fix, and assign an owner is often more dependable than one that presents attractive aggregate numbers with little context.

Turn findings into a working action plan

A performance review should end with a short written action plan. Limit it to the issues that will materially improve service, inventory control, cost, or speed. Each action needs an owner, due date, desired result, and a way to verify completion.

For example, repeated short shipments may require revised pick verification, clearer bin labels, or a product master-data check. Slow receiving may call for mandatory appointment scheduling, better advance shipping notices, or dedicated labor during scheduled container arrivals. The solution depends on the real cause, not the symptom.

Avoid demanding a new process for every isolated exception. Overcorrecting can slow the entire operation and add unnecessary handling cost. Focus first on repeated issues, high-value errors, and failures that affect customer commitments.

Set the right review cadence

Monthly reviews work well for most active fulfillment accounts because they catch trends before they become entrenched. Weekly operational check-ins may be necessary during a launch, peak season, system change, or recovery from a service issue. Quarterly reviews are useful for broader capacity planning, pricing assumptions, storage needs, and upcoming promotions.

The cadence should match the pace and risk of your operation. A business shipping a few pallets each month does not need the same reporting structure as an e-commerce brand dispatching hundreds of orders daily. But every account benefits from a defined communication rhythm and a clear route for urgent escalation.

At GrandMoor, the objective is straightforward: clients should know what is happening with their goods, understand the exceptions that need attention, and have a practical contact who can help keep their supply chain moving.

A fulfillment partner performance review is most valuable when it becomes a shared operating habit, not a once-a-year supplier score. The best result is not a perfect dashboard. It is a warehouse relationship where issues are visible early, responsibilities are clear, and your operation can grow without losing control.
