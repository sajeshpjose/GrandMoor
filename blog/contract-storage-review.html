Contract Storage Review: What to Check First

By Grandmoor Inc.

A contract storage review should begin before your first pallet arrives, not after inventory is already sitting in a warehouse. The right arrangement gives your team space, visibility, and room to grow. The wrong one can create unclear handling charges, slow order turnaround, inventory disputes, and a provider who becomes difficult to reach when a shipment is delayed.

For importers, e-commerce brands, wholesalers, and manufacturers, contract storage is more than leased square footage. It is an operating agreement that affects how quickly goods move from inbound freight to available inventory, how accurately orders leave the building, and how much control your team retains without running a warehouse of its own.

What a Contract Storage Review Should Cover

A useful review looks at the warehouse provider and the contract together. A well-run facility cannot compensate for vague service terms, and a detailed agreement has limited value if the operating team cannot deliver on it.

Start by defining the actual job the warehouse will perform. Some businesses only need pallet storage for reserve stock. Others need containers unloaded, inventory counted and labeled, orders picked and packed, retail routing followed, and carriers coordinated. These are very different operating requirements, even when both are described as "storage."

The scope should state what happens at each handoff: receiving, inspection, putaway, inventory updates, order release, picking, packing, shipping, returns, and reporting. If an activity is not addressed, ask whether it is included, billed separately, or unavailable. That direct conversation prevents assumptions from becoming surprise invoices.

Match the Storage Model to Your Inventory

Contract storage can be structured around shared space, dedicated space, or a hybrid approach. Shared pallet or shelf storage is often the practical choice for companies with changing volumes or seasonal demand. You pay for the capacity you use and avoid committing to empty space during slower periods.

Dedicated space provides more control when your inventory has consistent volume, special handling requirements, or a workflow that benefits from a fixed warehouse area. It may also support faster access for high-velocity products. The trade-off is commitment: dedicated capacity generally costs more and can be less forgiving if sales forecasts change.

A hybrid setup can make sense for a growing business. Core inventory can remain in assigned space while overflow moves into flexible storage. The key is to define how overflow is priced, when it applies, and whether additional capacity is available when you need it. A storage plan that works only at average volume is not a reliable plan.

Review Receiving and Inventory Control Closely

Inventory accuracy starts at the dock. Your agreement should explain how the warehouse receives freight, records quantities, identifies visible damage, and handles discrepancies against bills of lading, packing lists, or advance shipping notices.

Ask what happens when a container arrives with mixed SKUs, loose cartons, damaged pallets, or unexpected quantities. A provider should have a clear exception process, including who is notified, what documentation is supplied, and whether the goods are quarantined until your team provides direction. This matters especially for imported freight, where delays at receiving can increase storage pressure and disrupt downstream deliveries.

Your team also needs a practical view of stock after it is received. That does not always require a complicated technology project. It does require accurate inventory records, regular reporting, and a defined process for cycle counts and reconciliation. Review how often counts occur, how variances are investigated, and who has authority to approve adjustments.

For e-commerce and wholesale operations, ask whether inventory can be separated by SKU, lot, batch, expiration date, customer ownership, or sales channel where needed. A warehouse that stores products safely but cannot support the way you sell them may create more work for your internal team.

Look Beyond the Monthly Storage Rate

A low pallet rate can look attractive until handling costs begin to accumulate. Storage is only one component of the total logistics cost. A clear proposal should distinguish recurring storage charges from activity-based charges such as inbound unloading, palletizing, labeling, order picking, packing materials, outbound handling, returns, and special projects.

There is nothing wrong with activity-based pricing when it reflects real work. In fact, it can be a fair model for businesses with variable volumes. The issue is whether the rate structure is transparent enough for you to estimate your landed logistics cost before committing.

During your review, provide realistic operating data: average pallet count, monthly receipts, peak inbound periods, order volume, average lines per order, case or each-pick needs, and expected returns. Then ask the provider to walk through a normal month and a busy month. This gives you a more useful cost picture than comparing a single storage number.

Also review minimum charges, rate review timing, accessorial fees, and the notice period for price changes. If the agreement has a peak-season surcharge or long-term storage fee, it should be plainly defined. Predictability is often more valuable than a headline rate that cannot be modeled.

Confirm Service Levels and Escalation Paths

Storage contracts should set expectations for execution. Service-level agreements do not need to be overly complicated, but they should address the work that matters to your operation. Examples include receiving turnaround, inventory availability after receipt, order cut-off times, shipping turnaround, and the process for handling urgent requests.

A provider should be candid about what can be guaranteed and what depends on freight arrival times, customer order quality, carrier schedules, or unusually high volumes. Promises without operating limits are not useful. Defined targets, operating assumptions, and exception procedures are more dependable.

Ask who manages your account day to day. If there is an inventory issue at 3:00 p.m. or a retailer requires an urgent shipment, you should know who will respond and how decisions are made. Direct access to accountable people is especially valuable when your supply chain is moving across the Greater Toronto Area, Southwestern Ontario, or cross-border lanes.

GrandMoor approaches contract storage as an active operating relationship, with tailored terms, clear reporting, and hands-on account support rather than a black-box warehouse arrangement. That model is worth looking for in any provider: you should be able to reach someone who understands your goods and your priorities.

Check Facility Fit, Security, and Location

The building itself should fit the inventory and flow. Review racking configuration, dock access, ceiling clearance, staging space, loading procedures, and the ability to accommodate containers or scheduled carrier pickups. If you need value-added work such as labeling, kitting, repacking, or retailer preparation, confirm that the facility has both the space and labor process to perform it consistently.

Security and risk controls deserve the same level of attention. Ask about controlled access, camera coverage, fire protection, pest control, damaged-goods procedures, and insurance requirements. Confirm where responsibility changes hands and whether your own cargo or inventory insurance needs adjustment. A warehouse provider's coverage is not automatically a replacement for your policy.

Location is also operational, not just geographic. A site near major highways, airport cargo routes, rail terminals, ports, or your customer base can reduce transit time and carrier complexity. For businesses serving the GTA, access to Highway 401, 403, 407, the QEW, and Toronto Pearson can make a meaningful difference in inbound and outbound coordination. Still, proximity alone should not outweigh service quality and inventory discipline.

Know How the Agreement Starts and Ends

Before signing, review the onboarding plan. It should cover inventory transfer, SKU setup, labeling standards, system or reporting setup, initial count procedures, contacts, and the date the warehouse assumes responsibility. Rushed transitions are a common source of opening inventory errors.

The exit terms matter too. Check the contract length, renewal language, termination notice, removal timelines, and charges for pulling inventory out. A reasonable agreement protects both parties: the provider has enough notice to plan labor and space, while your business has a clear path to change capacity or move inventory if circumstances change.

The best contract storage decision is not the one with the most impressive brochure or the lowest quoted rate. It is the arrangement where space, handling, reporting, accountability, and cost all reflect how your goods actually move. When those details are clear at the start, your warehouse becomes a dependable extension of your operation instead of another problem your team has to chase.
