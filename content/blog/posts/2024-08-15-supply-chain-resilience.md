# Don't Bet Your Supply Chain on a Single Large 3PL

It's January 15th, 2024. A major 3PL in the US midwest experiences a network outage. Their WMS goes down. For 48 hours, they can't process orders from thousands of customers.

You're one of them. Your 3PL partner can't pick, pack, or ship orders. Your fulfillment stops. Your customers see "processing delay" emails. You're bleeding retention and chargeback fees.

By the time their system comes back online, they're swamped with a backlog. Your orders take another 72 hours to clear. In total, you've lost 4-5 days of order fulfillment in your peak selling season.

This happened. Thousands of e-commerce companies experienced it.

But here's the thing: **It should have been a non-event.**

If you had distributed your inventory across two or three regional warehouses—including a local partner—when one went down, the other 50-70% of your orders would have kept moving.

This is called supply chain resilience, and it's the most underrated competitive advantage in logistics.

## Why Concentration Risk Exists (And Why Most Companies Accept It)

Concentration risk in logistics happens for a simple reason: **It's cheaper and easier to have one partner.**

One master contract. One accounting relationship. One set of inventory records. One point of contact for exceptions. From an operational and financial perspective, consolidation looks smart.

Wall Street likes it too. Single-partner strategies reduce complexity, lower management overhead, and sometimes generate volume discounts. It looks efficient.

But efficiency and resilience are not the same thing. In fact, they're often inverse.

A highly efficient supply chain is optimized for perfect conditions: predictable demand, consistent supplier performance, and stable operations. Any deviation costs you money or creates delays.

A resilient supply chain is designed to handle deviations: demand spikes, supplier failures, facility outages, staffing issues, even natural disasters.

Most companies optimize for efficiency during good times and pray the bad times don't happen.

## The Real Cost of a Single 3PL

What's the actual cost if your sole 3PL partner goes down for 72 hours?

Let's model it for an e-commerce company doing $200k/month in revenue, with fulfillment handled by one 3PL:

- **Daily revenue:** $6,667
- **Outages:** 3 days (72 hours)
- **Revenue lost due to non-fulfillment:** $20,000 (assuming some customers accept delays; others request refunds)
- **Chargeback fees (customer disputes):** $2,000-5,000
- **Retained customer loss (30-day future value):** $5,000-10,000 (customers switch to competitors who can fulfill)
- **Rush freight to recover (if 3PL catches up backlog):** $3,000-5,000
- **Management time and disruption:** 40 hours of your team's time dealing with exceptions

**Total cost of 72-hour outage:** $30,000-45,000

For a company doing $200k/month, that's 1.5-2.25% of monthly revenue gone in one week.

This doesn't include reputational damage, lost reviews, or the operational stress of your team managing an outage.

And outages aren't rare. WMS failures, labor strikes, weather delays, cyberattacks, demand surges—any of these could impact your sole 3PL partner.

The probability of experiencing a 48-72 hour logistics disruption in any given year? For a company using one large 3PL, it's easily 10-20%.

## Why Large 3PLs Are More Vulnerable (Not Less)

Counterintuitively, using a large, "stable" 3PL doesn't reduce your risk. It often increases it.

Here's why:

**1. Consolidated Infrastructure**

Large 3PLs operate 5-10 mega-facilities serving tens of thousands of clients. If one facility has an outage, it affects 5,000+ customers simultaneously. Their IT teams are overwhelmed. Recovery takes longer because they're triaging thousands of support tickets.

A smaller, founder-led warehouse has 10-20 customers per facility. If something goes wrong, they're focused on fixing it for you, not managing 50 escalations.

**2. Shared Resources**

When a large 3PL experiences labor shortages, demand surges, or facility maintenance, they manage it by pulling staff from lower-priority clients (that's you). Your fulfillment slows down so they can handle their tier-1 customers (large box retailers, major brands).

A smaller partner has aligned incentives. Your growth is their growth. When demand surges, they invest in staffing. When maintenance is needed, they schedule it around your peak seasons.

**3. Systemic Risks**

Large 3PLs are vulnerable to systemic shocks:
- Ransomware attacks targeting their WMS (happened to major 3PLs multiple times)
- Staffing crises (unionization, turnover, wage competition)
- Facility consolidation (they close a facility, bump 5,000 small customers to a new one, operations degrade)
- Carrier issues (their contracted trucking partner raises rates or reduces service)

Smaller operators face fewer systemic risks because they're smaller. They're also more flexible. When a carrier raises rates, a founder-led warehouse has direct relationships to find alternatives quickly.

## The Resilience Strategy: Multi-Warehouse Approach

Here's what resilient e-commerce and logistics companies do:

**Distribute inventory across 2-3 regional warehouse partners:**

For a company selling across Canada:

- **Warehouse 1 (Ontario):** Handles GTA and Eastern Canada fulfillment (70% of volume)
- **Warehouse 2 (Western Hub):** Handles Western Canada fulfillment (20% of volume)
- **Warehouse 3 (Local/Backup):** Handles overflow, seasonal peaks, or emergency coverage (10% of volume)

When Warehouse 1 goes down, Warehouses 2 and 3 can redirect their inventory to fulfill 30% of customers immediately. By day 2, you've re-routed inventory from the other facilities. By day 3, you're back to 100% fulfillment capacity.

Total cost impact? Near-zero. Customers might see 1-2 day delays in peak season, but fulfillment continues.

## The Economics Actually Work

Here's the common objection: **"Multiple warehouses cost more."**

Not really. Here's why:

**Single Large 3PL Scenario:**
- Per-unit fulfillment: $1.50
- Monthly volume: 15,000 units
- Total: $22,500
- Plus: Occasional rush freight when 3PL is backlogged: +$2,000/month average
- Plus: Higher tier service (premium SLA to reduce risk): +$1,500/month
- **Total: $26,000/month**

**Multi-Warehouse Resilience Strategy:**
- Primary warehouse (70% volume): 10,500 units × $1.45 = $15,225
- Secondary warehouse (20% volume): 3,000 units × $1.50 = $4,500
- Tertiary/backup (10% volume): 1,500 units × $1.65 = $2,475
- Total fulfillment: $22,200
- No rush freight premiums: -$2,000
- Spread risk, so lower insurance/protection costs: -$500
- **Total: $19,700/month**

**Net savings: $6,300/month, plus operational resilience.**

The trick is that smaller, founder-led warehouses don't charge premium tier fees. They price based on volume handled, not "insurance" or "guaranteed SLA." Your cost per unit is actually lower than a large 3PL.

## How to Evaluate Multi-Warehouse Strategy

If you're currently single-sourced with a large 3PL, here's how to evaluate a multi-warehouse strategy:

1. **What's your geographic customer concentration?** (70% of orders from where?)
2. **What's your fulfillment volume per month?** (Can you split 70/20/10 or 50/50?)
3. **What's your current 3PL cost?** (Benchmark against multi-warehouse model)
4. **What's your recovery cost from a 72-hour outage?** (Calculate it honestly—it's usually $20k+)
5. **How often is your sole 3PL upgraded, maintained, or at risk?** (Ask directly—they won't volunteer this)

Once you've run the numbers, multi-warehouse strategy is often a no-brainer: lower cost + dramatically higher resilience.

## The Asymmetry

Here's the beautiful thing about supply chain resilience:

- **If everything works fine:** You save money with multiple smaller partners (lower per-unit costs)
- **If something goes wrong:** Multiple partners give you 70-80% operational continuity while you fix the issue

It's asymmetric upside. You win either way.

A single large 3PL? You only win in the perfect scenario.
